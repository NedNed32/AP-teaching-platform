Some indeterminate limits resist algebra, like $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^x - 1}{x}$$. L'Hospital's Rule replaces the top and bottom with their derivatives, which usually makes the limit easy.

## The rule

If $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 0$$ and $$\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 0$$, or both limits are infinite, then

$$
\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},
$$

provided the right-hand limit exists. The same holds for $$x \to \pm\infty$$.

## Why it works

Near $$a$$, each function is close to its tangent line: $$f(x) \approx f'(a)(x - a)$$ and $$g(x) \approx g'(a)(x - a)$$ when both are 0 at $$a$$. The factors $$(x - a)$$ cancel, leaving $$\frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}$$.

## Applying it

- Take derivatives of the numerator and denominator **separately**. This is not the quotient rule.
- If the new limit is still $$\frac{0}{0}$$ or $$\frac{\infty}{\infty}$$, apply the rule again.

<Note title="Show the indeterminate form">
The AP exam requires you to show that the limit has an indeterminate form before using L'Hospital's Rule, for example "$$\lim_{x \to 0} (1 - \cos x) = 0$$ and $$\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 = 0$$". Without that statement, the point is not earned.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Applying the rule twice">
Evaluate $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos 3x}{x^2}$$.

1. Numerator and denominator both approach 0, so the form is $$\frac{0}{0}$$.
2. First application: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{3\sin 3x}{2x}$$, still $$\frac{0}{0}$$.
3. Second application: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{9\cos 3x}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$$.

**Answer:** $$\frac{9}{2}$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

What is $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{e^{3x} - 1}{4x}$$?

**Answer:** $$\frac{3}{4}$$

**Why:** The limit has the form $$\frac{0}{0}$$, so L'Hospital's Rule applies: $$\frac{3e^{3x}}{4}$$.

**Working:** Direct substitution gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$. Differentiate the numerator and denominator separately (L'Hospital's Rule): $$\frac{3e^{3x}}{4}$$, which gives $$\frac{3}{4}$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$\frac{1}{4}$$: This leaves out the chain-rule factor when differentiating the numerator.
- $$\frac{4}{3}$$: This differentiates the numerator and denominator but then inverts the ratio, or applies the rule incorrectly.
- $$0$$: $$\frac{0}{0}$$ is indeterminate; it does not mean the limit is 0.
