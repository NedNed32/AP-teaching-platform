Limits at infinity describe the **end behavior** of a function: what happens far out to the right or left of the graph. When the outputs level off at a number, the graph has a horizontal asymptote.

## Rational functions: compare degrees

For $$\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$$ as $$x \to \pm\infty$$, only the highest-power terms matter:

| Degrees | Limit | Horizontal asymptote |
| --- | --- | --- |
| top lower than bottom | 0 | $$y = 0$$ |
| equal | ratio of leading coefficients | $$y = \frac{a}{b}$$ |
| top higher than bottom | $$\pm\infty$$ | none |

To show the work, divide every term by the highest power of $$x$$ in the denominator:

$$
\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2x + 1}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{2 + \frac{1}{x}}{1 - \frac{1}{x}} = \frac{2 + 0}{1 - 0} = 2.
$$

## Other end behaviors worth memorizing

- $$\lim_{x \to \infty} e^{-x} = 0$$ and $$\lim_{x \to -\infty} e^{x} = 0$$.
- $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \ln x = \infty$$, but slower than any power of $$x$$.
- $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 0$$ by the Squeeze Theorem, since $$-\frac{1}{x} \leq \frac{\sin x}{x} \leq \frac{1}{x}$$.

<Note title="Square roots and negative infinity">
For $$x \to -\infty$$, $$\sqrt{x^2} = |x| = -x$$. So $$\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{4x^2 + 1}}{x} = -2$$, while the limit at $$+\infty$$ is 2. A function can have two different horizontal asymptotes.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Dividing by the highest power">
Find $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2 - 5}{6x^2 + 4x}$$.

1. Divide every term by $$x^2$$: $$\frac{3 - \frac{5}{x^2}}{6 + \frac{4}{x}}$$.
2. As $$x \to \infty$$, the fractions with $$x$$ in the denominator approach 0.

**Answer:** $$\frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$, so $$y = \frac{1}{2}$$ is a horizontal asymptote.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

What is $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{-6x - 2}{2x^{2} + 6}$$?

**Answer:** $$0$$

**Why:** The denominator has the higher degree, so it outgrows the numerator and the fraction approaches 0.

**Working:** Dividing by $$x^2$$ gives $$\frac{-6/x - 2/x^2}{2 + 6/x^2}$$. The numerator approaches 0 and the denominator approaches $$2$$, so the limit is 0.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-3$$: Comparing leading coefficients only gives the limit when the degrees are equal.
- $$-\frac{1}{3}$$: This is the ratio of the constant terms, which only matters as x approaches 0.
- $$\infty$$: The denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the fraction shrinks toward 0.
