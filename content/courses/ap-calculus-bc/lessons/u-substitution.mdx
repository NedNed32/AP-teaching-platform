The chain rule produces derivatives like $$2x\cos(x^2)$$: an outer derivative evaluated at an inside function, times the inside's derivative. u-substitution recognizes that pattern and undoes it.

## The method

1. Choose $$u$$, usually the inside of a composite function.
2. Compute $$du = u'(x)\,dx$$.
3. Rewrite the whole integral in terms of $$u$$ and $$du$$. Adjust constant factors if needed.
4. Integrate in $$u$$.
5. Substitute back to $$x$$ (for indefinite integrals).

## Definite integrals: change the limits

With a definite integral, convert the limits to $$u$$-values and never go back to $$x$$:

$$
\int_0^2 x(x^2 + 1)^3\,dx \quad u = x^2 + 1,\; du = 2x\,dx \quad = \frac{1}{2}\int_1^5 u^3\,du = \frac{1}{8}u^4\Big|_1^5 = \frac{625 - 1}{8} = 78.
$$

## Fixing constants

If $$du$$ is off by a **constant**, compensate: with $$u = 3x$$, $$du = 3\,dx$$, so $$dx = \frac{1}{3}du$$. If it is off by a **variable** factor, the substitution does not work as chosen.

<Note title="Common choices for u">
The expression inside a power, root, exponential, logarithm or trig function; or a denominator whose derivative appears in the numerator, which leads to $$\ln|u|$$.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Substitution with a logarithm">
Find $$\int \frac{x}{x^2 + 4}\,dx$$.

1. $$u = x^2 + 4$$, $$du = 2x\,dx$$, so $$x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}du$$.
2. $$\frac{1}{2}\int \frac{1}{u}\,du = \frac{1}{2}\ln|u| + C$$.

**Answer:** $$\frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2 + 4) + C$$ (no absolute value needed since $$x^2 + 4 > 0$$).
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the facts and ideas the practice exams ask about this topic, each with the answer and the reason. If you can answer all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Facts and ideas the exams test

Cover the answers and test yourself.

**Q.** What is $$\int_0^{1} x\left(x^2 + 2\right)dx$$?

**A.** $$\frac{5}{4}$$. With $$u = x^2 + 2$$, $$du = 2x\,dx$$, the integral is $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{2}^{3} u\,du$$. $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{2}^{3} u\,du = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{u^{2}}{2}\Big|_{2}^{3} = \frac{9 - 4}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$$.

**Q.** What is $$\int_0^{1} x\left(x^2 + 2\right)^{3}dx$$?

**A.** $$\frac{65}{8}$$. With $$u = x^2 + 2$$, $$du = 2x\,dx$$, the integral is $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{2}^{3} u^{3}\,du$$. $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{2}^{3} u^{3}\,du = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{u^{4}}{4}\Big|_{2}^{3} = \frac{81 - 16}{8} = \frac{65}{8}$$.

**Q.** What is $$\int_0^{1} x\left(x^2 + 1\right)^{3}dx$$?

**A.** $$\frac{15}{8}$$. With $$u = x^2 + 1$$, $$du = 2x\,dx$$, the integral is $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2} u^{3}\,du$$. $$\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2} u^{3}\,du = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{u^{4}}{4}\Big|_{1}^{2} = \frac{16 - 1}{8} = \frac{15}{8}$$.

**Q.** What is $$\int_0^{\pi/6} \sin(6x)\,dx$$?

**A.** $$\frac{1}{3}$$. $$-\frac{1}{6}\cos(6x)\Big|_0^{\pi/6} = -\frac{1}{6}(-1 - 1) = \frac{2}{6}$$. $$\int \sin(6x)\,dx = -\frac{1}{6}\cos(6x) + C$$. Evaluating: $$-\frac{1}{6}\left[\cos\pi - \cos 0\right] = \frac{2}{6}$$.
