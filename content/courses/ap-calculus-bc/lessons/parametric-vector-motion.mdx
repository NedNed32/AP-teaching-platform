Free-response Question 2 on the BC exam is often a particle moving in the plane, given by $$\frac{dx}{dt}$$ and $$\frac{dy}{dt}$$. Every part draws on a small set of formulas.

## The toolkit

| Question | Compute |
| --- | --- |
| Slope of the path | $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y'(t)}{x'(t)}$$ |
| Speed | $$\sqrt{x'(t)^2 + y'(t)^2}$$ |
| Acceleration vector | $$\langle x''(t), y''(t) \rangle$$ |
| Position at time $$b$$ | $$x(a) + \int_a^b x'\,dt$$ and $$y(a) + \int_a^b y'\,dt$$ |
| Total distance | $$\int_a^b \sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}\,dt$$ |
| Moving left or right | sign of $$x'(t)$$ |
| Moving up or down | sign of $$y'(t)$$ |

## Speeding up in the plane

Speed is increasing when the derivative of speed is positive. With a calculator, evaluate the derivative of $$\sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}$$ numerically, or check whether the velocity and acceleration vectors point in roughly the same direction (positive dot product).

<Note title="Displacement versus distance">
The change in position is the vector $$\left\langle \int x'\,dt, \int y'\,dt \right\rangle$$. The distance travelled integrates the **speed**. They are different questions with different answers.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="A calculator motion problem">
A particle has $$x'(t) = \cos(t^2)$$ and $$y'(t) = e^{0.5t}$$, with $$(x(0), y(0)) = (2, 1)$$. Find its speed at $$t = 1$$ and its total distance for $$0 \leq t \leq 1$$.

1. Speed: $$\sqrt{\cos^2 1 + e^{1}} \approx \sqrt{0.2919 + 2.7183} \approx 1.735$$.
2. Distance: $$\int_0^1 \sqrt{\cos^2(t^2) + e^{t}}\,dt$$, evaluated on a calculator.

**Answer:** speed $$\approx 1.735$$; distance $$\approx 1.595$$ (calculator).
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A particle has velocity $$\vec{v}(t) = \langle 2t - 2, 3t^{2} \rangle$$ and position $$(-2, 2)$$ at $$t = 0$$. Where is the particle at $$t = 3$$?

**Answer:** $$(1, 29)$$

**Why:** Add the integral of each velocity component to the starting coordinate.

**Working:** $$x(3) = -2 + \int_0^{3} (2t - 2)\,dt = 1$$ and $$y(3) = 2 + \int_0^{3} (3t^{2})\,dt = 29$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$(3, 27)$$: This is the displacement; add the starting position.
- $$(4, 27)$$: This is the velocity at the final time, not the position.
- $$(2, 29)$$: This adds the final velocity instead of the accumulated change in position.

### Free-response practice: Motion in the plane

A particle moves in the xy-plane so that for $$0 \leq t \leq 4$$, $$\frac{dx}{dt} = 3\sin\left(\frac{t^2}{3}\right) + 1$$ and $$\frac{dy}{dt} = \cos(t)\,e^{t/4}$$. At $$t = 0$$ the particle is at $$(-2, -1)$$.

**(a)** Find the speed of the particle and its acceleration vector at $$t = 3.5$$.

To earn the point: Gives the speed. Model answer: $$\sqrt{x'(3.5)^2 + y'(3.5)^2} \approx 2.661$$

To earn the point: Gives the acceleration vector. Model answer: $$\langle x''(3.5), y''(3.5) \rangle \approx \langle -4.119, 0.280 \rangle$$

**(b)** Find the slope of the line tangent to the path of the particle at $$t = 3.5$$.

To earn the point: Divides the velocity components. Model answer: $$\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \approx 1.576$$
