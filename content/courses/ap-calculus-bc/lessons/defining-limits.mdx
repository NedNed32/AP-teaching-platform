How fast is a car going *at* one instant? Speed is distance divided by time, but in a single instant no time passes and no distance is covered, so the formula gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$. Calculus gets around this with one idea, the **limit**. Instead of asking what happens *at* a point, it asks what happens as you get *closer and closer* to it.

## Getting close without arriving

Take $$f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$$. At $$x = 1$$ the formula gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$, so $$f(1)$$ is undefined. But look at inputs near 1:

| $$x$$ | 0.9 | 0.99 | 0.999 | 1.001 | 1.01 | 1.1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $$f(x)$$ | 1.9 | 1.99 | 1.999 | 2.001 | 2.01 | 2.1 |

As $$x$$ gets closer to 1 from either side, $$f(x)$$ gets closer to 2. We write

$$
\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = 2
$$

and read it as "the limit of $$f(x)$$ as $$x$$ approaches 1 is 2". The function never has to *equal* 2, and it doesn't even have to be defined at 1. A limit is about the trend near the point, not the value at it.

## Reading the notation

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$$ says: you can make $$f(x)$$ as close to $$L$$ as you like by taking $$x$$ close enough to $$a$$, but not equal to $$a$$. Each piece has a job:

- $$x \to a$$ names the input and the point it approaches.
- $$f(x)$$ is the function whose outputs you watch.
- $$L$$ is the number those outputs approach. It must be a single, finite number.

## One-sided limits

Sometimes the two sides of a point behave differently, so we look at each side on its own:

- $$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$$ is the **left-hand limit**: $$x$$ approaches $$a$$ through values less than $$a$$.
- $$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$$ is the **right-hand limit**: $$x$$ approaches $$a$$ through values greater than $$a$$.

The two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist and are equal:

$$
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad\text{exactly when}\quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \text{ and } \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L.
$$

<Note title="The limit is not the function's value">
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$$ and $$f(a)$$ answer different questions. The limit describes the outputs *near* $$a$$; $$f(a)$$ is the output *at* $$a$$. They can be equal or different, and either one can exist without the other.
</Note>

## When a limit does not exist

A limit fails to exist when the outputs don't settle on one finite number. There are three common ways this happens:

1. **The sides disagree.** For $$g(x) = \frac{|x|}{x}$$, the output is $$-1$$ for every negative $$x$$ and $$1$$ for every positive $$x$$. The one-sided limits at 0 are $$-1$$ and $$1$$, so $$\lim_{x \to 0} g(x)$$ does not exist.
2. **The outputs grow without bound.** As $$x \to 0$$, $$\frac{1}{x^2}$$ becomes arbitrarily large, so there is no finite limit. Writing $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^2} = \infty$$ describes *how* the limit fails to exist (topic 1.14).
3. **The outputs oscillate.** As $$x \to 0$$, $$\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$$ swings between $$-1$$ and $$1$$ infinitely often and never settles.

<WorkedExample title="Reading one-sided limits">
Let $$h(x) = x + 1$$ for $$x < 2$$, and $$h(x) = 5 - x$$ for $$x \geq 2$$. Does $$\lim_{x \to 2} h(x)$$ exist?

1. From the left, $$h(x) = x + 1$$, which approaches $$2 + 1 = 3$$.
2. From the right, $$h(x) = 5 - x$$, which approaches $$5 - 2 = 3$$.
3. Both one-sided limits equal 3.

**Answer:** $$\lim_{x \to 2} h(x) = 3$$. Here $$h(2) = 3$$ as well, but the limit would still be 3 if $$h(2)$$ were defined as something else.
</WorkedExample>

<Analogy breaksDown="A photo has a smallest pixel, so zooming in eventually stops. In a limit there is no last step: x can always get closer to a.">
A limit is like zooming in on a photo around one point. As you zoom in, you ignore the single pixel in the middle and look at what everything around it is closing in on.
</Analogy>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

Let $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 3x - 2 & x < 1 \\ -3x - 6 & x \geq 1 \end{cases}$$. What is $$\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$$?

**Answer:** The limit does not exist.

**Why:** The left-hand limit is $$1$$ and the right-hand limit is $$-9$$. They differ, so the two-sided limit does not exist.

**Working:** From the left, $$f(x) = 3x - 2 \to 1$$. From the right, $$f(x) = -3x - 6 \to -9$$. Since $$1 \neq -9$$, $$\lim_{x \to 1} f(x)$$ does not exist.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-9$$: This is $$f(1)$$ and the right-hand limit only. The left-hand limit is $$1$$.
- $$1$$: This is the left-hand limit only. The right-hand limit is $$-9$$.
- $$-4$$: A limit is never the average of the one-sided limits. When they differ, the limit does not exist.
