Let a Taylor polynomial's degree grow without bound and you get a Taylor series. For the functions in this course, the series actually equals the function on its interval of convergence.

## Series to memorize

| Function | Maclaurin series | Converges for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $$e^x$$ | $$\sum \frac{x^n}{n!} = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots$$ | all $$x$$ |
| $$\sin x$$ | $$\sum \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n + 1)!} = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \cdots$$ | all $$x$$ |
| $$\cos x$$ | $$\sum \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots$$ | all $$x$$ |
| $$\frac{1}{1 - x}$$ | $$\sum x^n = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots$$ | $$\lvert x\rvert < 1$$ |

## Building new series from old

Rather than computing derivatives, substitute into a known series:

$$
e^{-x^2} = 1 - x^2 + \frac{x^4}{2!} - \frac{x^6}{3!} + \cdots, \qquad x\sin x = x^2 - \frac{x^4}{3!} + \cdots.
$$

Multiply by powers of $$x$$, substitute expressions for $$x$$, or differentiate and integrate term by term (topic 10.15).

<Note title="General term">
Many free-response questions ask for the **general term**. Include the $$(-1)^n$$ sign pattern, the power of $$x$$ and the factorial, and check it against the first few terms.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Series by substitution">
Find the first four nonzero terms and the general term of the Maclaurin series for $$\cos(2x)$$.

1. Substitute $$2x$$ into the cosine series: $$1 - \frac{(2x)^2}{2!} + \frac{(2x)^4}{4!} - \frac{(2x)^6}{6!}$$.
2. Simplify: $$1 - 2x^2 + \frac{2}{3}x^4 - \frac{4}{45}x^6$$.

**Answer:** $$1 - 2x^2 + \frac{2}{3}x^4 - \frac{4}{45}x^6 + \cdots$$, with general term $$\frac{(-1)^n (2x)^{2n}}{(2n)!}$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A function $$f$$ has $$f(0) = 4$$, $$f'(0) = -6$$, $$f''(0) = -3$$ and $$f'''(0) = -5$$. What is the coefficient of $$x^3$$ in the Taylor polynomial for $$f$$ about $$x = 0$$?

**Answer:** $$-\frac{5}{6}$$

**Why:** $$\frac{f'''(0)}{3!} = \frac{-5}{6}$$.

**Working:** The coefficient of $$x^k$$ is $$\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}$$; for $$k = 3$$ it is $$\frac{-5}{6} = -\frac{5}{6}$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-5$$: This forgets to divide by $$3!$$.
- $$-\frac{5}{3}$$: This divides by 3 instead of $$3! = 6$$.
- $$-\frac{3}{2}$$: This is the coefficient of the squared term.

### Worked exam question 2

A function $$f$$ has $$f(3) = 3$$, $$f'(3) = -6$$ and $$f''(3) = 2$$. Using the second-degree Taylor polynomial for $$f$$ about $$x = 3$$, what is the approximation of $$f(2.9)$$?

**Answer:** $$3.610$$

**Why:** $$P_2(2.9) = 3 + (-6)(-0.1) + \frac{2}{2}(-0.1)^2$$.

**Working:** $$P_2(x) = 3 - 6(x - 3) + \frac{2}{2}(x - 3)^2$$, evaluated at $$x = 2.9$$: $$3.610$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$3.620$$: This forgets to divide the second derivative by $$2!$$.
- $$3.600$$: This is the tangent-line (first-degree) approximation.
- $$3.500$$: The quadratic term must use $$(x - a)^2$$, not $$(x - a)$$.
