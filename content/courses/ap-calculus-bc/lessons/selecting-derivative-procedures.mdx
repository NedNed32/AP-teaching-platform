By now you have every derivative rule in the course. The remaining skill is choosing the right one, which comes down to reading the structure of an expression.

## Find the outermost operation

Ask: *if I computed this for a specific $$x$$, what would I do last?*

| Last step | Rule to start with |
| --- | --- |
| Add or subtract terms | Differentiate term by term |
| Multiply two expressions | Product rule |
| Divide two expressions | Quotient rule (or rewrite) |
| Apply a function to an expression | Chain rule |
| Equation in $$x$$ and $$y$$ | Implicit differentiation |
| Inverse of a known function | $$\frac{1}{f'(a)}$$ |

Then apply the same question to each piece until everything is a basic derivative.

## Rewrite to save work

- $$\frac{5}{(2x + 1)^3} = 5(2x + 1)^{-3}$$: chain rule instead of quotient rule.
- $$\ln\left(\frac{x^2}{x + 1}\right) = 2\ln x - \ln(x + 1)$$: log rules first.
- $$\frac{x^2 + x}{x} = x + 1$$ for $$x \neq 0$$.

<Note title="Simplify only when asked">
On free-response questions you do not need to simplify a derivative unless the question requires it, for example to find where it equals zero. Unsimplified correct work earns full credit.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Several rules in one">
Differentiate $$y = x^2 e^{3x}$$.

1. Outermost operation: a product of $$x^2$$ and $$e^{3x}$$, so start with the product rule.
2. $$\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x$$; $$\frac{d}{dx}e^{3x} = 3e^{3x}$$ by the chain rule.
3. $$y' = 2x e^{3x} + x^2 \cdot 3e^{3x}$$.

**Answer:** $$y' = xe^{3x}(2 + 3x)$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

The table gives values of the differentiable functions $$f$$ and $$g$$ and their derivatives.

*Values of f, f′, g and g′*

| $$x$$ | $$f(x)$$ | $$f'(x)$$ | $$g(x)$$ | $$g'(x)$$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 6 | 5 | 3 | -1 |
| 2 | -2 | -3 | 4 | 2 |
| 3 | 4 | 3 | 4 | -1 |
| 4 | 6 | 4 | 4 | 2 |

Let $$h(x) = f(g(x))$$. What is $$h'(3)$$?

**Answer:** $$-4$$

**Why:** $$h'(3) = f'(g(3)) \cdot g'(3) = f'(4) \cdot (-1) = (4)(-1)$$.

**Working:** Chain rule: $$h'(x) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$$. Since $$g(3) = 4$$, $$h'(3) = f'(4)\,g'(3) = -4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-3$$: This evaluates $$f'$$ at $$3$$ instead of at $$g(3) = 4$$.
- $$4$$: This leaves out the factor $$g'(3)$$ from the chain rule.
- $$-6$$: This uses $$f$$ instead of $$f'$$ for the outer derivative.

**Q.** What is $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\cos^{5} x\right)$$?

**A.** $$-5\cos^{4} x \sin x$$. This is the chain rule: the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inside, times the derivative of the inside. Treat $$\cos^{5} x$$ as a composite and apply the chain rule: $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\cos^{5} x\right) = -5\cos^{4} x \sin x$$.
