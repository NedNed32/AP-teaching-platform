Many laws of nature describe how fast something changes rather than what it is: populations grow in proportion to their size, hot coffee cools faster when it is much hotter than the room. Such statements are **differential equations**.

## Translating words into equations

| Statement | Differential equation |
| --- | --- |
| rate of change of $$y$$ is proportional to $$y$$ | $$\frac{dy}{dt} = ky$$ |
| rate is proportional to the square of $$y$$ | $$\frac{dy}{dt} = ky^2$$ |
| rate is inversely proportional to $$y$$ | $$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{k}{y}$$ |
| rate is proportional to the difference between $$y$$ and 50 | $$\frac{dy}{dt} = k(y - 50)$$ |

## Reading a differential equation

A differential equation gives the slope of the solution at every point. Without solving it you can still answer:

- **Is $$y$$ increasing?** Check the sign of $$\frac{dy}{dt}$$ at the point.
- **Is the solution concave up?** Differentiate the equation implicitly to get $$\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}$$, then check its sign.
- **Where is $$y$$ constant?** Where $$\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$ for all $$t$$ (equilibrium solutions).

<Note title="Second derivatives from the equation">
For $$\frac{dy}{dx} = x + y$$, differentiate both sides: $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = 1 + \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 + x + y$$. Remember that $$y$$ depends on $$x$$, so its derivative is $$\frac{dy}{dx}$$, not 0.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Increasing and concavity at a point">
A solution of $$\frac{dy}{dx} = xy - 2$$ passes through $$(1, 3)$$. Is it increasing there, and is it concave up?

1. $$\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \cdot 3 - 2 = 1 > 0$$: increasing.
2. $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = y + x\frac{dy}{dx} = 3 + 1 \cdot 1 = 4 > 0$$: concave up.

**Answer:** increasing and concave up at $$(1, 3)$$.
</WorkedExample>
