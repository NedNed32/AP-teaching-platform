A speedometer shows a speed *right now*, yet every formula for speed you have used so far needs two moments: distance divided by elapsed time. Calculus begins by asking how change can be measured at a single instant.

## From average to instantaneous

Suppose a ball is dropped and has fallen $$s(t) = 16t^2$$ feet after $$t$$ seconds. Its **average speed** between $$t = 1$$ and $$t = 2$$ is

$$
\frac{s(2) - s(1)}{2 - 1} = \frac{64 - 16}{1} = 48 \text{ feet per second}.
$$

To learn the speed *at* $$t = 1$$, shrink the interval:

| Interval | Average speed (ft/s) |
| --- | --- |
| $$[1, 2]$$ | 48 |
| $$[1, 1.1]$$ | 33.6 |
| $$[1, 1.01]$$ | 32.16 |
| $$[1, 1.001]$$ | 32.016 |

The averages close in on 32. We say the **instantaneous speed** at $$t = 1$$ is 32 feet per second. The process of watching values approach a target is called a **limit**, and it is the single idea every topic in this course is built on.

## The two big questions

Calculus answers two questions that look different but turn out to be linked:

1. **The tangent problem.** What is the slope of a curve at a single point? This is the instantaneous rate of change, and it leads to the **derivative** (Units 2 to 5).
2. **The area problem.** How much area lies under a curve, or how much total change builds up from a rate? This leads to the **integral** (Units 6 to 8).

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Unit 6) shows that each problem undoes the other.

<Note title="Why limits are needed">
Plugging $$t = 1$$ into the average-speed formula over $$[1, 1]$$ gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$. A limit avoids dividing by zero by asking what the averages approach as the interval shrinks, without ever using an interval of length zero.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Estimating a rate at an instant">
The volume of water in a tank is $$V(t) = t^3$$ liters after $$t$$ minutes. Estimate how fast the volume is changing at $$t = 2$$.

1. Over $$[2, 2.1]$$: $$\frac{2.1^3 - 8}{0.1} = \frac{9.261 - 8}{0.1} = 12.61$$.
2. Over $$[2, 2.01]$$: $$\frac{8.120601 - 8}{0.01} = 12.0601$$.
3. Over $$[2, 2.001]$$: about $$12.006$$.

**Answer:** the averages approach 12, so the volume is increasing at about 12 liters per minute at $$t = 2$$.
</WorkedExample>

<Analogy breaksDown="A photo has a finite number of frames, but a limit uses intervals that keep shrinking forever without reaching zero.">
Think of a video of a moving car. One frame shows no motion at all; two frames a second apart show an average. Taking two frames closer and closer together gives a better and better picture of the speed at one moment.
</Analogy>

## On the practice exams

The practice exams do not ask about this topic on its own, but it supports these questions from the same unit. Work through them to see how the ideas are used.

### Worked exam question 1

What is $$\lim_{x \to -5} \frac{x^{2} + 6x + 5}{x + 5}$$?

**Answer:** $$-4$$

**Why:** The numerator factors as $$(x + 5)(x + 1)$$. Cancel $$x + 5$$ and substitute: $$-5 - (-1) = -4$$.

**Working:** Direct substitution gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$, so factor: $$x^{2} + 6x + 5 = (x + 5)(x + 1)$$. For $$x \neq -5$$ the expression equals $$x + 1$$, so the limit is $$-5 - (-1) = -4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$0$$: Substituting gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$, an indeterminate form, not the value 0.
- $$-6$$: This adds the roots. After cancelling, the expression is $$x + 1$$, which approaches $$-4$$.
- The limit does not exist.: The expression is undefined at the point, but its outputs nearby approach a single number, so the limit exists.

### Worked exam question 2

What is $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{-3x - 8}{x^{2} + 5}$$?

**Answer:** $$0$$

**Why:** The denominator has the higher degree, so it outgrows the numerator and the fraction approaches 0.

**Working:** Dividing by $$x^2$$ gives $$\frac{-3/x - 8/x^2}{1 + 5/x^2}$$. The numerator approaches 0 and the denominator approaches $$1$$, so the limit is 0.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-3$$: Comparing leading coefficients only gives the limit when the degrees are equal.
- $$-\frac{8}{5}$$: This is the ratio of the constant terms, which only matters as x approaches 0.
- $$\infty$$: The denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the fraction shrinks toward 0.

### Worked exam question 3

Let $$f(x) = \begin{cases} x - 5 & x < -1 \\ 2x + 3 & x \geq -1 \end{cases}$$. What is $$\lim_{x \to -1^-} f(x)$$?

**Answer:** $$-6$$

**Why:** For $$x < -1$$, $$f(x) = x - 5$$, which approaches $$-6$$.

**Working:** The left-hand limit uses the piece for $$x < -1$$: $$(-1) - 5 = -6$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$1$$: This is the right-hand limit (and $$f(-1)$$). The left-hand limit uses the formula for $$x < -1$$.
- The limit does not exist.: One-sided limits can exist even when the two-sided limit does not.
- $$-5$$: This value doesn't come from substituting the approach point into the left-hand formula.
