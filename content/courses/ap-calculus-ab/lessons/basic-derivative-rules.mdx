Derivatives respect addition and scaling. That means you can differentiate a polynomial one term at a time.

## The rules

| Rule | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Constant | $$\frac{d}{dx} c = 0$$ |
| Constant multiple | $$\frac{d}{dx}[c f(x)] = c f'(x)$$ |
| Sum | $$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) + g(x)] = f'(x) + g'(x)$$ |
| Difference | $$\frac{d}{dx}[f(x) - g(x)] = f'(x) - g'(x)$$ |

Combined with the power rule:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(5x^3 - 4x^2 + 7x - 2\right) = 15x^2 - 8x + 7.
$$

## Simplify before differentiating

Products and quotients of simple terms can often be expanded or split first, avoiding the product and quotient rules:

$$
\frac{x^3 + 2x}{x} = x^2 + 2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dx} = 2x \quad (x \neq 0).
$$

<Note title="No such rule for products">
The derivative of a product is **not** the product of the derivatives: $$\frac{d}{dx}(x \cdot x) = 2x$$, but $$1 \cdot 1 = 1$$. Products need the product rule (topic 2.8).
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Horizontal tangents">
Where does $$f(x) = x^3 - 12x + 5$$ have horizontal tangent lines?

1. $$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12$$.
2. Set $$f'(x) = 0$$: $$3x^2 = 12$$, so $$x^2 = 4$$.

**Answer:** at $$x = -2$$ and $$x = 2$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

If $$f(x) = -3x^{3} + 3x^{2} - x - 7$$, what is $$f'(-1)$$?

**Answer:** $$-16$$

**Why:** $$f'(x) = -9x^{2} + 6x - 1$$, so $$f'(-1) = -16$$.

**Working:** By the power rule, $$f'(x) = -9x^{2} + 6x - 1$$. Substituting $$x = -1$$ gives $$-16$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$0$$: This is $$f(-1)$$, the value of the function, not its derivative.
- $$-15$$: This drops the derivative of the linear term, which is its coefficient.
- $$-23$$: The derivative of a constant is 0; this keeps the constant term.

### Worked exam question 2

If $$f(x) = 4e^{x} + 6\ln x - x^{2}$$, what is $$f'(1)$$?

**Answer:** $$4e + 4$$

**Why:** $$f'(x) = 4e^{x} + \frac{6}{x} - 2x$$, so $$f'(1) = 4e + 4$$.

**Working:** Differentiate term by term: $$f'(x) = 4e^{x} + \frac{6}{x} - 2x$$. At $$x = 1$$ this is $$4e + 4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$4e - 2$$: This uses $$\ln 1 = 0$$, but the derivative of $$\ln x$$ is $$\frac{1}{x}$$, which is 1 at $$x = 1$$.
- $$4e + 5$$: The derivative of $$-x^2$$ is $$-2x$$.
- $$8$$: The derivative of $$e^x$$ is $$e^x$$, which equals $$e$$ at 1, not 1.
