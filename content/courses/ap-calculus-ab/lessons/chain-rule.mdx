Most functions you meet are built by plugging one function into another: $$\sin(x^2)$$, $$e^{3x}$$, $$\sqrt{1 + x^2}$$. The chain rule says how fast such a composite changes: multiply the rates of each link in the chain.

## The rule

$$
\frac{d}{dx} f(g(x)) = f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
$$

Differentiate the **outer** function, leaving the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the **inside**.

In Leibniz notation, with $$u = g(x)$$ and $$y = f(u)$$:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}.
$$

## Common patterns

| Function | Derivative |
| --- | --- |
| $$[g(x)]^n$$ | $$n[g(x)]^{n - 1} g'(x)$$ |
| $$e^{g(x)}$$ | $$e^{g(x)} g'(x)$$ |
| $$\ln g(x)$$ | $$\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$$ |
| $$\sin g(x)$$ | $$\cos g(x) \cdot g'(x)$$ |
| $$\cos g(x)$$ | $$-\sin g(x) \cdot g'(x)$$ |

<Note title="Layers can stack">
For $$\sin^3(2x)$$, there are three layers: cube, sine, and $$2x$$. Peel them from the outside in: $$3\sin^2(2x) \cdot \cos(2x) \cdot 2$$.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Chain rule from a table">
Given $$g(1) = 3$$, $$g'(1) = -2$$, and $$f'(3) = 5$$, find $$h'(1)$$ for $$h(x) = f(g(x))$$.

1. $$h'(1) = f'(g(1)) \cdot g'(1)$$.
2. $$g(1) = 3$$, so $$f'(g(1)) = f'(3) = 5$$.

**Answer:** $$h'(1) = 5 \cdot (-2) = -10$$.
</WorkedExample>

<Analogy breaksDown="Gear ratios are constant, while the rates in the chain rule usually change from point to point.">
Linked gears: if gear A turns 3 times for each turn of B, and B turns 2 times for each turn of C, then A turns 6 times for each turn of C. The chain rule multiplies rates the same way.
</Analogy>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

If $$f(x) = (x^{2} - 2)^{2}$$, what is $$f'(1)$$?

**Answer:** $$-4$$

**Why:** $$f'(x) = 2(x^{2} - 2) \cdot (2x)$$.

**Working:** Chain rule: $$f'(x) = 2(x^{2} - 2) \cdot (2x)$$. At $$x = 1$$: $$2(-1) \cdot (2) = -4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-2$$: This differentiates the outer power but leaves out the derivative of the inside function.
- $$1$$: This is the value of the function, not its derivative.
- $$4$$: This applies the power rule to the derivative of the inside instead of to the inside function.

### Worked exam question 2

The table gives values of the differentiable functions $$f$$ and $$g$$ and their derivatives.

*Values of f, f′, g and g′*

| $$x$$ | $$f(x)$$ | $$f'(x)$$ | $$g(x)$$ | $$g'(x)$$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 4 | -1 | 4 | -3 |
| 2 | -3 | 3 | 4 | 2 |
| 3 | 3 | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| 4 | 0 | -5 | 1 | 4 |

Let $$h(x) = f(g(x))$$. What is $$h'(4)$$?

**Answer:** $$-4$$

**Why:** $$h'(4) = f'(g(4)) \cdot g'(4) = f'(1) \cdot (4) = (-1)(4)$$.

**Working:** Chain rule: $$h'(x) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$$. Since $$g(4) = 1$$, $$h'(4) = f'(1)\,g'(4) = -4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-20$$: This evaluates $$f'$$ at $$4$$ instead of at $$g(4) = 1$$.
- $$-1$$: This leaves out the factor $$g'(4)$$ from the chain rule.
- $$16$$: This uses $$f$$ instead of $$f'$$ for the outer derivative.

**Q.** What is $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\cos^{2} x\right)$$?

**A.** $$-2\cos^{1} x \sin x$$. This is the chain rule: the derivative of the outer function, evaluated at the inside, times the derivative of the inside. Treat $$\cos^{2} x$$ as a composite and apply the chain rule: $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\cos^{2} x\right) = -2\cos^{1} x \sin x$$.
