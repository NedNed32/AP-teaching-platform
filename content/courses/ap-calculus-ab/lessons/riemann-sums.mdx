To find the area under a curved graph, chop it into thin vertical strips and approximate each strip with a shape whose area you know.

<Figure src="assets/lessons/left-riemann.svg" alt="An increasing curve with five rectangles beneath it, each with height equal to the curve's value at its left edge." caption="A left Riemann sum for an increasing function sits below the curve." />

## Four approximations

Split $$[a, b]$$ into subintervals. On each one, approximate the area by:

| Method | Height or shape |
| --- | --- |
| Left | rectangle with height at the left endpoint |
| Right | rectangle with height at the right endpoint |
| Midpoint | rectangle with height at the midpoint |
| Trapezoidal | trapezoid joining the endpoint heights |

With equal widths $$\Delta x$$, the trapezoidal sum is the average of the left and right sums.

## Unequal widths from tables

Free-response tables often have uneven spacing. Compute each piece with its own width: $$\text{width} \times \text{height}$$ for rectangles, or $$\frac{\text{width}}{2}(\text{left} + \text{right})$$ for trapezoids.

## Over or under?

| Function behavior | Left sum | Right sum | Trapezoidal sum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| increasing | under | over | depends on concavity |
| decreasing | over | under | depends on concavity |
| concave up | depends on direction | depends on direction | over |
| concave down | depends on direction | depends on direction | under |

<Note title="Midpoints from tables">
A midpoint sum needs values at the middle of each subinterval. With a table, the subintervals for a midpoint sum are usually twice as wide, so that the table values sit at their centers.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Trapezoids with uneven widths">
Estimate $$\int_0^{10} r(t)\,dt$$ with a trapezoidal sum, given:

| $$t$$ | 0 | 2 | 7 | 10 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $$r(t)$$ | 4 | 6 | 9 | 5 |

1. $$\frac{2}{2}(4 + 6) = 10$$.
2. $$\frac{5}{2}(6 + 9) = 37.5$$.
3. $$\frac{3}{2}(9 + 5) = 21$$.

**Answer:** $$10 + 37.5 + 21 = 68.5$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

Water flows into a tank at a rate of $$R(t)$$ gallons per minute, where $$R$$ is a continuous function of time $$t$$ in minutes. Selected values are shown.

*Rate of flow R(t)*

| $$t$$ (minutes) | $$R(t)$$ (gallons per minute) |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 10 |
| 2 | 6 |
| 3 | 5 |
| 7 | 5 |
| 10 | 1 |

Using a right Riemann sum with the four subintervals indicated by the table, what is the approximation of $$\int_0^{10} R(t)\,dt$$?

**Answer:** $$40$$

**Why:** Multiply each subinterval's width by the appropriate height and add.

**Working:** The widths are 2, 1, 4, 3. Use the right endpoint of each subinterval, multiply by the width and add: $$40$$ gallons.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$61$$: This is the left Riemann sum.
- $$\frac{101}{2}$$: This is the trapezoidal sum.
- $$52$$: This treats every subinterval as width 2, but the subintervals have unequal widths.

### Free-response practice: Rate of people entering a park

People enter an amusement park at a rate modeled by a differentiable function $$P$$, where $$P(t)$$ is measured in people per minute and $$t$$ is measured in minutes. Selected values are shown.

*Values of P(t)*

| $$t$$ (minutes) | $$P(t)$$ (people per minute) |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 60 |
| 3 | 71 |
| 5 | 40 |
| 9 | 71 |
| 12 | 77 |

**(a)** Use the data to estimate $$P'(7)$$. Show the computations and indicate units.

To earn the point: Uses a difference quotient on the interval containing the time. Model answer: $$\frac{P(9) - P(5)}{9 - 5} = \frac{31}{4}$$

To earn the point: Gives correct units. Model answer: people per minute per minute

**(b)** Use a trapezoidal sum with the four subintervals shown in the table to approximate $$\int_0^{12} P(t)\,dt$$. Explain the meaning of this integral in the context of the problem.

To earn the point: Sets up the trapezoidal sum with unequal widths. Model answer: $$\frac{3}{2}(60 + 71)$$ + $$\frac{2}{2}(71 + 40)$$ + $$\frac{4}{2}(40 + 71)$$ + $$\frac{3}{2}(71 + 77)$$

To earn the point: Gives the correct value. Model answer: $$\frac{1503}{2}$$

To earn the point: Interprets the integral with units. Model answer: The total number of people who enter the park during the first 12 minutes.

**(c)** Must there be a time $$c$$, for $$3 < c < 9$$, such that $$P'(c) = 0$$? Justify your answer.

To earn the point: Answers yes, citing the Mean Value Theorem (or Rolle's Theorem) with its conditions. Model answer: Yes. $$P$$ is differentiable (so continuous), and $$P(3) = P(9) = 71$$. By the Mean Value Theorem there is a $$c$$ with $$P'(c) = \frac{71 - 71}{6} = 0$$.

**(d)** Must there be a time $$t$$, for $$0 < t < 3$$, at which $$P(t) = 69$$? Justify your answer.

To earn the point: Answers yes, citing the Intermediate Value Theorem and continuity. Model answer: Yes. $$P$$ is continuous, and $$69$$ is between $$P(0) = 60$$ and $$P(3) = 71$$, so the IVT guarantees such a $$t$$.
