When two changing quantities are multiplied, both of them contribute to how fast the product changes. The product rule accounts for each contribution.

## The rule

$$
(fg)' = f'g + fg'
$$

In words: *derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times derivative of the second.*

## Why it works

Think of $$f(x)g(x)$$ as the area of a rectangle with sides $$f$$ and $$g$$. When $$x$$ increases a little, the area gains a strip $$\Delta f \cdot g$$ along one side, a strip $$f \cdot \Delta g$$ along the other, and a tiny corner $$\Delta f \cdot \Delta g$$. Dividing by $$\Delta x$$ and letting $$\Delta x \to 0$$, the corner vanishes, leaving $$f'g + fg'$$.

## Three factors

Apply the rule twice: $$(fgh)' = f'gh + fg'h + fgh'$$. Each term differentiates one factor and keeps the others.

<Note title="Product rule with tables">
Free-response questions often give a table of $$f$$, $$f'$$, $$g$$, $$g'$$ values. Write the rule first, then substitute; graders look for the correct structure as much as the final number.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="A product with an exponential">
Find $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^3 e^x\right)$$ and simplify.

1. $$f = x^3$$, $$f' = 3x^2$$; $$g = e^x$$, $$g' = e^x$$.
2. $$3x^2 e^x + x^3 e^x$$.

**Answer:** $$x^2 e^x(3 + x)$$.
</WorkedExample>

**Q.** If $$f(x) = x^{2} e^{x}$$, what is $$f'(1)$$?

**A.** $$3e$$. $$f'(x) = 2x^{1}e^x + x^{2}e^x$$, so $$f'(1) = 2e + e = 3e$$. By the product rule, $$f'(x) = 2x^{1}e^x + x^{2}e^x = x^{1}e^x(x + 2)$$. At $$x = 1$$, $$f'(1) = e(1 + 2) = 3e$$.
