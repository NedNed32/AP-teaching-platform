A graph lets you *see* a limit: trace the curve toward the input you care about, once from the left and once from the right, and read the height you are heading toward.

<Figure src="assets/lessons/limits-from-graph.svg" alt="A graph with a line rising to an open circle at (1, 3.5), a closed dot at (1, 1), and a downward parabola from x = 1 to 5 with an open circle at (3, 3) and a closed dot at (3, 0). There is also an open circle on the line at (-2, 2)." caption="Open circles are holes; closed dots show the function's value." />

## Reading the graph above

Call the function $$f$$.

- **At $$x = -2$$** there is a hole. Both sides of the line head to height 2, so $$\lim_{x \to -2} f(x) = 2$$, even though $$f(-2)$$ is undefined.
- **At $$x = 1$$** the left piece heads to 3.5 and the right piece heads to 1. So $$\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 3.5$$, $$\lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = 1$$, and the two-sided limit does not exist. The closed dot shows $$f(1) = 1$$.
- **At $$x = 3$$** the parabola heads to 3 from both sides, so $$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 3$$. The closed dot below shows $$f(3) = 0$$: the limit and the value disagree.

## A routine for any graph

1. Put your finger on the curve to the **left** of the point and slide toward it. Note the height: that is the left-hand limit.
2. Do the same from the **right**.
3. If both heights are the same finite number, that number is the two-sided limit.
4. Separately, look for a closed dot directly above or below the point. That gives the function value, which may differ from the limit or not exist.

<Note title="Graphs can mislead">
Calculator graphs can hide holes, because a single missing point is too small to draw. A hand-drawn or textbook graph marks it with an open circle; on a calculator screen you may need to check algebraically or with a table.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Limits and values together">
Use the graph above. Find $$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) + f(1)$$.

1. Both sides of $$x = 3$$ approach height 3, so $$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 3$$.
2. The closed dot at $$x = 1$$ is at height 1, so $$f(1) = 1$$.

**Answer:** $$3 + 1 = 4$$.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

The practice exams do not ask about this topic on its own, but it supports these questions from the same unit. Work through them to see how the ideas are used.

### Worked exam question 1

What is $$\lim_{x \to -5} \frac{x^{2} + 6x + 5}{x + 5}$$?

**Answer:** $$-4$$

**Why:** The numerator factors as $$(x + 5)(x + 1)$$. Cancel $$x + 5$$ and substitute: $$-5 - (-1) = -4$$.

**Working:** Direct substitution gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$, so factor: $$x^{2} + 6x + 5 = (x + 5)(x + 1)$$. For $$x \neq -5$$ the expression equals $$x + 1$$, so the limit is $$-5 - (-1) = -4$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$0$$: Substituting gives $$\frac{0}{0}$$, an indeterminate form, not the value 0.
- $$-6$$: This adds the roots. After cancelling, the expression is $$x + 1$$, which approaches $$-4$$.
- The limit does not exist.: The expression is undefined at the point, but its outputs nearby approach a single number, so the limit exists.

### Worked exam question 2

What is $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{-3x - 8}{x^{2} + 5}$$?

**Answer:** $$0$$

**Why:** The denominator has the higher degree, so it outgrows the numerator and the fraction approaches 0.

**Working:** Dividing by $$x^2$$ gives $$\frac{-3/x - 8/x^2}{1 + 5/x^2}$$. The numerator approaches 0 and the denominator approaches $$1$$, so the limit is 0.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$-3$$: Comparing leading coefficients only gives the limit when the degrees are equal.
- $$-\frac{8}{5}$$: This is the ratio of the constant terms, which only matters as x approaches 0.
- $$\infty$$: The denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the fraction shrinks toward 0.

### Worked exam question 3

Let $$f(x) = \begin{cases} x - 5 & x < -1 \\ 2x + 3 & x \geq -1 \end{cases}$$. What is $$\lim_{x \to -1^-} f(x)$$?

**Answer:** $$-6$$

**Why:** For $$x < -1$$, $$f(x) = x - 5$$, which approaches $$-6$$.

**Working:** The left-hand limit uses the piece for $$x < -1$$: $$(-1) - 5 = -6$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$1$$: This is the right-hand limit (and $$f(-1)$$). The left-hand limit uses the formula for $$x < -1$$.
- The limit does not exist.: One-sided limits can exist even when the two-sided limit does not.
- $$-5$$: This value doesn't come from substituting the approach point into the left-hand formula.
