$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

**Independent** events: $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$, equivalently $$P(A \mid B) = P(A)$$.

Subtracting $$P(A \cap B)$$ prevents counting outcomes in both events twice.

<Note title="Independence is checked, not assumed">
Use data or the setup of the process to decide. Repeated coin flips are independent; drawing without replacement from a small group is not.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="General addition rule">
40% of students take Spanish, 30% take art, and 12% take both. What fraction take Spanish or art? Are the events independent?

1. 0.40 + 0.30 − 0.12 = 0.58.
2. 0.40 × 0.30 = 0.12 = P(both).

**Answer:** 0.58; yes, they are independent.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.2 and P(A and B) = 0.16. What is P(A or B), and are A and B independent?

**Answer:** 0.54; not independent

**Why:** P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B); independent if P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) = 0.1.

**Working:** 0.5 + 0.2 − 0.16 = 0.54. P(A)P(B) = 0.1 ≠ 0.16.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 0.7; not independent: Subtract P(A and B) so the overlap is not counted twice.
- 0.54; independent: Compare P(A and B) with P(A)·P(B) = 0.1.
- 0.54; mutually exclusive: P(A and B) is not 0, so they are not mutually exclusive.

### Worked exam question 2

Each of 6 independent devices fails with probability 0.02. What is the probability that at least one fails?

**Answer:** 0.1142

**Why:** 1 − P(none fail) = 1 − (1 − p)^n.

**Working:** 1 − 0.98^6 = 0.1142.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 0.12: Probabilities of overlapping events cannot simply be added.
- 0: That is the probability all fail.
- 0.8858: That is the probability none fail.
