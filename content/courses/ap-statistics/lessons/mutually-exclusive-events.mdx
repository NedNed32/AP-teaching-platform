For mutually exclusive events: $$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

Mutually exclusive is **not** the same as independent. Disjoint events are strongly dependent: knowing A happened tells you B did not.

<Note title="Check before adding">
Only add probabilities directly when the events cannot overlap.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Disjoint events">
A student's grade is A with probability 0.25 and B with probability 0.40. What is P(A or B)?

1. A grade cannot be both A and B.

**Answer:** 0.65.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

P(A) = 0.5, P(B) = 0.2 and P(A and B) = 0.16. What is P(A or B), and are A and B independent?

**Answer:** 0.54; not independent

**Why:** P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B); independent if P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) = 0.1.

**Working:** 0.5 + 0.2 − 0.16 = 0.54. P(A)P(B) = 0.1 ≠ 0.16.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 0.7; not independent: Subtract P(A and B) so the overlap is not counted twice.
- 0.54; independent: Compare P(A and B) with P(A)·P(B) = 0.1.
- 0.54; mutually exclusive: P(A and B) is not 0, so they are not mutually exclusive.
