## Passive transport

Molecules move from high to low concentration (down the gradient), with no energy from the cell. This includes **simple diffusion** and **facilitated diffusion**.

## Active transport

Proteins move substances **against** their gradient using energy, usually ATP. Example: the **sodium–potassium pump**, which moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, building gradients used by nerve cells.

## Bulk transport

- **Endocytosis**: the membrane folds inward to bring material in (phagocytosis for particles, pinocytosis for fluid, receptor-mediated for specific molecules).
- **Exocytosis**: vesicles fuse with the membrane to release contents.

Both use energy.

<Note title="Gradients store energy">
An active pump builds a gradient; that gradient can then power other transport (cotransport) or signaling.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Identify the process">
A cell's internal K⁺ concentration is 30 times higher than outside, yet it keeps taking in K⁺. When ATP production is blocked, uptake stops. What process is this?

1. K⁺ moves against its gradient.
2. It depends on ATP.

**Answer:** active transport.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A plant cell has a water potential of -0.94 MPa. It is placed in a solution with a water potential of -0.1 MPa. Which describes the net movement of water?

**Answer:** Into the cell, because water moves toward the lower (more negative) water potential

**Why:** Water moves from -0.1 MPa to -0.94 MPa.

**Working:** Water flows from higher to lower water potential: from the solution into the cell.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- Out of the cell, because the cell has the lower water potential: Water moves from higher to lower water potential.
- No net movement, because water potential does not affect osmosis: Differences in water potential drive osmosis.
- Into the cell, because water moves toward higher water potential: Water moves toward lower water potential.

### Free-response practice: Osmosis in potato tissue

Students placed potato cores of equal mass in sucrose solutions of different concentrations at 22 °C. After 24 hours, they measured each core's percent change in mass.

*Percent change in mass*

| Sucrose (M) | Change in mass (%) |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 18.8 |
| 0.2 | 7.56 |
| 0.4 | -2.69 |
| 0.6 | -13.47 |
| 0.8 | -23.45 |

**(a)** Identify an appropriate control for the experiment, and explain why the students measured percent change rather than change in mass.

To earn the point: The 0 M (distilled water) treatment, or cores with no solution change. Model answer: Distilled water.

To earn the point: Percent change allows comparison of cores with slightly different starting masses. Model answer: It standardizes for initial mass.

**(b)** Estimate the sucrose concentration that is isotonic to the potato cells, and calculate the solute potential of that solution (Ψs = −iCRT, R = 0.0831, T in kelvins).

To earn the point: Isotonic concentration where the change in mass is zero. Model answer: About 0.35 M, interpolating between 0.2 and 0.4 M.

To earn the point: Solute potential calculated correctly from the estimate. Model answer: Ψs = −(1)(0.35)(0.0831)(295) ≈ -8.58 bars.

**(c)** Explain the change in mass of the cores in the 0.8 M solution.

To earn the point: Water moved out of the cells by osmosis toward the lower water potential of the solution. Model answer: The solution is hypertonic, so the cells lost water and mass.
