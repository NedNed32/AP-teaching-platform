In the short run, firms produce more when the price level rises. The reason is that output prices adjust faster than the costs of inputs, especially wages.

## Why SRAS slopes upward

Nominal wages are often fixed by contracts. When the price level rises but wages do not, each unit of output is more profitable, so firms expand production. Some prices are also sticky, and firms may mistake general inflation for higher demand for their own product.

## SRAS shifters

| Shift | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| Left (decrease) | higher input prices (oil, wages), higher expected inflation, business taxes or regulation, a natural disaster |
| Right (increase) | lower input prices, lower expected inflation, subsidies, productivity gains |

A rise in expected inflation shifts SRAS left because workers bargain for higher nominal wages.

<Note title="Productivity shifts both">
A lasting rise in productivity (better technology, more capital) shifts both SRAS and LRAS right. A temporary input price change shifts only SRAS.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Classifying an event">
A drought cuts the harvest and food processors pay more for crops. Which curve shifts, and what happens to the price level and real GDP in the short run?

1. Higher input costs: SRAS shifts left.
2. Along an unchanged AD, the price level rises and real GDP falls.

**Answer:** SRAS shifts left, causing stagflation: higher prices and lower output.
</WorkedExample>
