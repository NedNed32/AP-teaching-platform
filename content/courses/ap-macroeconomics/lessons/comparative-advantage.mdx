Why should a country that is better at producing everything still trade? Because what matters for trade is not who is *best* at a good, but who gives up the *least* to produce it.

## Absolute versus comparative

- **Absolute advantage:** more output from the same resources.
- **Comparative advantage:** lower **opportunity cost**.

Specialization according to comparative advantage, followed by trade, lets both parties consume beyond their own PPCs.

## Output versus input problems

| Data given | Opportunity cost of good X |
| --- | --- |
| **Output** (units produced per worker or day) | $$\frac{\text{output of Y}}{\text{output of X}}$$, the "other over" rule |
| **Input** (hours needed per unit) | $$\frac{\text{hours for X}}{\text{hours for Y}}$$, the "IOU" rule: input, own on top |

## Terms of trade

A trade price benefits both sides when it lies **between** their opportunity costs. If shirts cost 2 bananas in country X and 5 in country Y, X should export shirts, and any price between 2 and 5 bananas per shirt helps both.

<Note title="Compare opportunity costs, not outputs">
On the exam, a country with an absolute advantage in both goods is the classic trap. Always compute the opportunity costs before deciding who specializes in what.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Who specializes?">
In a day, Aria can produce 12 pies or 4 cakes. Ben can produce 6 pies or 3 cakes. Who has the comparative advantage in cakes?

1. Aria: 1 cake costs $$\frac{12}{4} = 3$$ pies.
2. Ben: 1 cake costs $$\frac{6}{3} = 2$$ pies.
3. Ben gives up fewer pies per cake.

**Answer:** Ben has the comparative advantage in cakes (Aria has it in pies), even though Aria has the absolute advantage in both.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

The table shows the maximum output per worker per day in two countries.

*Output per worker per day*

| Country | bicycles | chairs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X | 11 | 2 |
| Y | 6 | 4 |

Which of the following is true?

**Answer:** Country X has a comparative advantage in bicycles, and Country Y in chairs.

**Why:** One unit of bicycles costs 0.18 chairs in X and 0.67 chairs in Y.

**Working:** Compare opportunity costs, not output levels. In X, one bicycles costs $$\frac{2}{11}$$ chairs; in Y, $$\frac{2}{3}$$ chairs. The country with the lower cost has the comparative advantage in bicycles; the other has it in chairs.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- Country Y has a comparative advantage in bicycles, and Country X in chairs.: This reverses the comparison of opportunity costs.
- Country X has a comparative advantage in both goods.: No country can have a comparative advantage in both goods; opportunity costs are reciprocals.
- Neither country can gain from trade.: Different opportunity costs always allow mutually beneficial trade.

### Worked exam question 2

The table shows the maximum output per worker per day in two countries.

*Output per worker per day*

| Country | cars | computers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| X | 12 | 8 |
| Y | 5 | 5 |

Which of the following terms of trade, in units of computers per unit of cars, would benefit both countries?

**Answer:** $$\frac{5}{6}$$

**Why:** It lies between the two opportunity costs of cars: $$\frac{2}{3}$$ and $$1$$.

**Working:** Mutually beneficial terms of trade lie strictly between the countries' opportunity costs of cars, here $$\frac{2}{3}$$ and $$1$$ units of computers.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$\frac{1}{3}$$: This is below both opportunity costs; the exporter of the good would lose.
- $$2$$: This is above both opportunity costs; the importer would lose.
- $$\frac{17}{13}$$: Terms of trade come from opportunity costs, not total outputs.
