$$E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx} \qquad \text{uniform field: } E = \frac{\Delta V}{d}$$

- Field lines are perpendicular to equipotentials.
- The field points toward **decreasing** potential.
- Closely spaced equipotentials mean a strong field.
- Moving a charge along an equipotential takes no work.

<Note title="Units">
Electric field can be written in V/m, which is the same as N/C.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="From a graph">
V drops from 120 V to 40 V over 0.20 m in a uniform field. Find E.

1. $$E = 80/0.20 = 400$$ V/m.

**Answer:** 400 V/m, pointing toward lower potential.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

The electric potential along the x-axis is $$V(x) = 70x^3$$ V. What is the x-component of the electric field at $$x = 1.5$$ m?

**Answer:** $$-473\ \mathrm{V/m}$$

**Why:** E_x = −dV/dx.

**Working:** E_x = −210x² = -473 V/m.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$473\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: E = −dV/dx: include the minus sign.
- $$-236\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: Differentiate V.
- $$-88.6\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: Differentiate, do not integrate.

### Worked exam question 2

Two parallel plates 0.07 m apart have a potential difference of 160 V. What is the field between them?

**Answer:** $$2290\ \mathrm{V/m}$$

**Why:** In a uniform field, E = ΔV/d.

**Working:** E = 160/0.07 = 2290 V/m.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$11.2\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: Divide by the separation.
- $$3.27 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: E = V/d.
- $$4.38 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{V/m}$$: The ratio is inverted.
