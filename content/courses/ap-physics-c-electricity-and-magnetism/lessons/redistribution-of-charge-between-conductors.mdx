Connected conductors share charge until their potentials are equal. For two distant spheres:

$$\frac{kQ_1}{R_1} = \frac{kQ_2}{R_2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{Q_1}{Q_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

The smaller sphere gets less charge but a higher surface charge density (and field), since $$\sigma \propto 1/R$$.

Total charge is conserved: $$Q_1 + Q_2 = Q_{total}$$.

<Note title="Lightning rods">
This is why fields are strongest at sharp points: they behave like tiny spheres at the same potential as the rest of the conductor.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Sharing charge">
A sphere of radius 0.10 m with 9.0 µC is connected to an uncharged sphere of radius 0.20 m. Find the final charges.

1. $$Q_1/Q_2 = 1/2$$ and $$Q_1 + Q_2 = 9.0$$ µC.

**Answer:** 3.0 µC and 6.0 µC.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

Two identical small conducting spheres carry 5 µC and -6 µC. They are touched together and separated. What is the charge on each?

**Answer:** -0.5 µC

**Why:** Identical conductors share the total charge equally.

**Working:** Total -1 µC, split equally: -0.5 µC each.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- -1 µC: The total is shared between two spheres.
- 5.5 µC: Add the charges with their signs.
- 5 µC and -6 µC: Charge flows between touching conductors.

### Worked exam question 2

A conducting sphere of radius 3 cm carrying 48 nC is connected by a long thin wire to an uncharged conducting sphere of radius 5 cm far away. What charge ends up on the smaller sphere?

**Answer:** 18 nC

**Why:** Connected spheres reach equal potential, so Q ∝ R.

**Working:** kQ₁/R₁ = kQ₂/R₂ with Q₁ + Q₂ = 48: Q₁ = 18 nC.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 24 nC: The spheres have different radii.
- 30 nC: That is the larger sphere's charge.
- 12.7 nC: Equal potential gives Q ∝ R, not R².
