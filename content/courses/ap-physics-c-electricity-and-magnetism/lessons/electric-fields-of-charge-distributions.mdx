Break the distribution into elements $$dq$$ and integrate:

$$\vec E = \int \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}\hat r$$

Use symmetry to cancel components first.

| Distribution | Field |
| --- | --- |
| ring (radius a) on its axis at x | $$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qx}{(x^2 + a^2)^{3/2}}$$ |
| infinite line | $$\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$ |
| infinite sheet | $$\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$ |
| rod on its axis, distance d from end | $$\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{d(d + L)}$$ |

<Note title="Check limits">
Far from any distribution, its field should look like a point charge's. For a ring, when x is much larger than a, E approaches kQ/x².
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Rod on its axis">
A rod of length L and charge Q lies from x = 0 to x = L. Find E at x = L + d.

1. $$dE = \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{(L + d - x)^2}$$.
2. $$E = k\lambda\left[\frac{1}{d} - \frac{1}{L + d}\right] = \frac{kQ}{d(L + d)}$$.

**Answer:** $$\frac{kQ}{d(L+d)}$$, pointing away from the rod for positive Q.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A ring of radius 0.14 m carries 3 µC spread uniformly. What is the field magnitude on its axis 0.3 m from the center?

**Answer:** $$2.23 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

**Why:** E = kQx/(x² + a²)^(3/2).

**Working:** E = kQx/(x² + a²)^(3/2) = 2.23 \times 10^\{5\} N/C.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$2.46 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: Only the axial components add; the others cancel.
- $$3 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: The charge is not at the center.
- $$7.38 \times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: The denominator has a 3/2 power.

### Worked exam question 2

A long straight wire carries a uniform charge of 9 µC/m. What is the field 0.21 m from it?

**Answer:** $$7.71 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

**Why:** E = λ/(2πε₀r), from Gauss's law with a cylinder.

**Working:** E = λ/(2πε₀r) = 7.71 \times 10^\{5\} N/C.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$1.83 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: A line charge's field falls off as 1/r.
- $$3.85 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: The cylinder gives 2πrL, not 4πr².
- $$5.08 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: That is a sheet's field.
