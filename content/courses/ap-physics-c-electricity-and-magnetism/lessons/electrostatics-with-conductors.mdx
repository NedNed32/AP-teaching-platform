In electrostatic equilibrium, a conductor has:

- $$E = 0$$ everywhere inside;
- all excess charge on its surface;
- the same potential throughout (an equipotential);
- a field just outside that is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude $$\sigma/\varepsilon_0$$.

Charge concentrates where the surface curves most sharply (points), so the field is strongest there.

A charge placed inside a cavity of a conducting shell induces an equal and opposite charge on the cavity's wall.

<Note title="Shielding">
A closed conductor shields its interior from external fields, which is why a car is safe in a lightning strike.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Shell with a charge inside">
A neutral conducting shell surrounds a +5 µC point charge. What charges appear on its inner and outer surfaces?

1. E = 0 in the metal, so the inner surface has −5 µC.
2. The shell is neutral, so the outer surface has +5 µC.

**Answer:** −5 µC inside, +5 µC outside.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A conducting sphere of radius 3 cm carrying 48 nC is connected by a long thin wire to an uncharged conducting sphere of radius 5 cm far away. What charge ends up on the smaller sphere?

**Answer:** 18 nC

**Why:** Connected spheres reach equal potential, so Q ∝ R.

**Working:** kQ₁/R₁ = kQ₂/R₂ with Q₁ + Q₂ = 48: Q₁ = 18 nC.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 24 nC: The spheres have different radii.
- 30 nC: That is the larger sphere's charge.
- 12.7 nC: Equal potential gives Q ∝ R, not R².
