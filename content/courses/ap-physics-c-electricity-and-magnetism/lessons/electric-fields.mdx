$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q} \qquad E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|q|}{r^2}$$

The field points away from positive charges and toward negative ones. A charge $$q$$ in a field feels $$\vec F = q\vec E$$: a negative charge is pushed opposite the field.

Fields from several charges add as vectors. Between two equal like charges, the field is zero at the midpoint.

<Note title="Uniform fields">
Between large parallel plates the field is uniform, so a charge accelerates with constant a = qE/m, like a projectile.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Zero field">
A +4q charge is at x = 0 and a +q charge at x = 3 m. Where between them is E = 0?

1. $$\frac{4q}{x^2} = \frac{q}{(3-x)^2}$$.
2. $$2(3 - x) = x$$, so $$x = 2$$ m.

**Answer:** x = 2 m.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

Charges of +6 µC and +3 µC are 0.4 m apart. What is the magnitude of the electric field at the midpoint between them?

**Answer:** $$6.74 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$

**Why:** Like charges' fields point in opposite directions at the midpoint: subtract.

**Working:** Each field is k q/(d/2)²: 1.35 \times 10^\{6\} and 6.74 \times 10^\{5\} N/C, opposite, giving 6.74 \times 10^\{5\} N/C.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$2.02 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: Check the directions of the two fields at the midpoint.
- $$5.06 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: Each charge is d/2 from the midpoint.
- $$1.69 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/C}$$: The distance is d/2, so r² = d²/4.
