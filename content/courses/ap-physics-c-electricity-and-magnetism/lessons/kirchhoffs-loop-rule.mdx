$$\sum \Delta V = 0 \text{ around any loop}$$

Sign conventions while walking around a loop:

- Through a resistor **with** the current: $$-IR$$; against it: $$+IR$$.
- Through a battery from − to +: $$+\varepsilon$$; from + to −: $$-\varepsilon$$.

If a solved current comes out negative, it simply flows opposite the direction you assumed.

<Note title="Pick directions freely">
Assume any direction for each unknown current. The algebra corrects the sign.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Opposing batteries">
A 12 V and a 4 V battery oppose each other in a single loop with a total resistance of 4 Ω. Find the current.

1. $$12 - 4 - I(4) = 0$$.

**Answer:** 2 A, in the direction driven by the 12 V battery.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A single loop contains a 10 V battery and a 2 V battery connected so their emfs oppose each other, plus resistors of 3 Ω and 5 Ω. What is the current?

**Answer:** $$1\ \mathrm{A}$$

**Why:** Loop rule: ε₁ − ε₂ − I(R₁ + R₂) = 0.

**Working:** I = (10 − 2)/(3 + 5) = 1 A.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$1.5\ \mathrm{A}$$: The batteries oppose each other.
- $$1.25\ \mathrm{A}$$: Include the opposing battery.
- $$2.67\ \mathrm{A}$$: Both resistors are in the loop.
