$$L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{q}{C} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad q = Q_{max}\cos(\omega t + \phi) \qquad \omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$$

Energy is conserved:

$$\frac{Q_{max}^2}{2C} = \frac{q^2}{2C} + \frac12 LI^2 \qquad I_{max} = \omega Q_{max}$$

| Spring–mass | LC circuit |
| --- | --- |
| x | q |
| v | I |
| m | L |
| k | 1/C |

<Note title="Same math, different physics">
Recognizing the same differential equation lets you reuse everything you know about simple harmonic motion.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Frequency">
Find the oscillation frequency of a circuit with L = 10 mH and C = 4.0 µF.

1. $$\omega = 1/\sqrt{(0.010)(4.0 \times 10^{-6})} = 5000$$ rad/s.
2. $$f = 5000/2\pi$$.

**Answer:** about 800 Hz.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

An LC circuit has a 23 mH inductor and a 4 µF capacitor. What is its frequency f of oscillation?

**Answer:** $$525\ \mathrm{Hz}$$

**Why:** ω = 1/√(LC), f = ω/(2π).

**Working:** ω = 1/√(LC) = 3300 rad/s; f = 525 Hz.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$3300\ \mathrm{Hz}$$: That is ω; divide by 2π.
- $$1.73 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{Hz}$$: Take the square root of LC.
- $$1.53\ \mathrm{Hz}$$: Convert mH and µF; ω = 1/√(LC).

### Worked exam question 2

An LC circuit has a 36 mH inductor and a 22 µF capacitor. What is its angular frequency ω of oscillation?

**Answer:** $$1120\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

**Why:** ω = 1/√(LC), f = ω/(2π).

**Working:** ω = 1/√(LC) = 1120 rad/s; f = 179 Hz.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$179\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: That is f; ω = 2πf.
- $$1.26 \times 10^{6}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: Take the square root.
- $$40.5\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: ω = 1/√(LC).
