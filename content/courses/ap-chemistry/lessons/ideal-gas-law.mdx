$$PV = nRT \qquad R = 0.08206\ \text{L·atm/(mol·K)}$$

Temperature must be in kelvins. Combined forms: $$\frac{P_1V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$$.

**Dalton's law**: $$P_{total} = P_A + P_B + \cdots$$, and $$P_A = X_A P_{total}$$ where $$X_A$$ is the mole fraction.

Molar mass from gas data: $$M = \frac{mRT}{PV}$$.

<Note title="Kelvin always">
Add 273 to °C. Using Celsius gives wrong (sometimes negative) answers.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Moles of gas">
How many moles of gas are in a 5.00 L flask at 2.00 atm and 27 °C?

1. $$n = PV/RT = (2.00)(5.00)/(0.08206 \times 300)$$.

**Answer:** 0.406 mol.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

What is the pressure of 0.87 mol of gas in a 38 L container at 305 K?

**Answer:** $$0.573\ \mathrm{atm}$$

**Why:** P = nRT/V.

**Working:** P = (0.87)(0.08206)(305)/38 = 0.573 atm.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$0.0601\ \mathrm{atm}$$: Temperature is already in kelvins.
- $$6.98\ \mathrm{atm}$$: Include R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).
- $$58.1\ \mathrm{atm}$$: With L and atm, use R = 0.08206.

### Worked exam question 2

What volume does 1.42 mol of gas occupy at 2.43 atm and 417 K?

**Answer:** $$20\ \mathrm{L}$$

**Why:** V = nRT/P.

**Working:** V = nRT/P = 20 L.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$1.15 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{L}$$: V = nRT/P.
- $$31.8\ \mathrm{L}$$: 22.4 L/mol applies only at 0 °C and 1 atm.
- $$2030\ \mathrm{L}$$: Use R = 0.08206 with atm and L.

### Worked exam question 3

How many moles of gas are in a 13 L flask at 0.618 atm and 408 K?

**Answer:** $$0.24\ \mathrm{mol}$$

**Why:** n = PV/RT.

**Working:** n = PV/RT = 0.24 mol.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$269\ \mathrm{mol}$$: n = PV/(RT): divide by RT.
- $$0.0197\ \mathrm{mol}$$: Include R.
- $$0.58\ \mathrm{mol}$$: Conditions are not 0 °C and 1 atm.

### Worked exam question 4

A gas mixture contains 0.9 mol N₂, 0.6 mol O₂ and 0.2 mol Ar at a total pressure of 4.1 atm. What is the partial pressure of N₂?

**Answer:** $$2.17\ \mathrm{atm}$$

**Why:** P(N₂) = X(N₂) × P(total).

**Working:** X = 0.9/1.7 = 0.529; P = 2.17 atm.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$3.69\ \mathrm{atm}$$: Use the mole fraction, not moles.
- $$1.37\ \mathrm{atm}$$: The gases are not present in equal amounts.
- $$4.61\ \mathrm{atm}$$: Divide by total moles.

### Free-response practice: Decomposing calcium carbonate

A student heats 4.9 g of $$\ce{CaCO3(s)}$$, which decomposes completely: $$\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$$. The $$\ce{CO2}$$ is collected at 297 K and 1.03 atm.

**(a)** Calculate the number of moles of CO₂ produced.

To earn the point: Correct moles using molar mass 100.09 g/mol and the 1:1 ratio. Model answer: 4.9 ÷ 100.09 = 0.049 mol

**(b)** Calculate the volume of CO₂ expected.

To earn the point: Correct use of PV = nRT. Model answer: V = nRT/P = (0.049)(0.08206)(297)/1.03 = 1.16 L

**(c)** The student measures 1.043 L of gas. Calculate the percent yield, and give one experimental reason the yield could be less than 100%.

To earn the point: Percent yield. Model answer: 1.043 ÷ 1.16 × 100% = 90%

To earn the point: A valid reason. Model answer: Incomplete decomposition (not heated long enough) or gas leaking from the apparatus.

**(d)** Draw (describe) a complete Lewis diagram for CO₂, and identify its molecular geometry and the hybridization of carbon.

To earn the point: Correct Lewis diagram. Model answer: O=C=O with two lone pairs on each O (16 valence electrons).

To earn the point: Linear geometry, sp hybridization. Model answer: Two electron domains on C.

**(e)** Explain why CO₂ is nonpolar even though C=O bonds are polar.

To earn the point: Linear symmetry makes the bond dipoles cancel. Model answer: Equal and opposite dipoles at 180°.

**(f)** CaO is an ionic solid. Explain, using Coulomb's law, why CaO has a higher melting point than KCl.

To earn the point: Larger ion charges in CaO (2+/2−) than in KCl (1+/1−). Model answer: Charges are double.

To earn the point: Greater electrostatic attraction (and smaller ions) means more energy is needed to separate the ions. Model answer: Attraction ∝ q₁q₂/r².
