At the equivalence point, moles of titrant relate to moles of analyte by the balanced equation.

For a 1 : 1 reaction: $$M_aV_a = M_bV_b$$.

Sources of error: overshooting the endpoint (too much titrant, overestimating the analyte), or not rinsing the buret with titrant (diluted titrant, more volume needed).

<Note title="Check the ratio">
For H₂SO₄ with NaOH the ratio is 1 : 2, so M_aV_a ≠ M_bV_b.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Diprotic titration">
20.0 mL of H₂SO₄ requires 30.0 mL of 0.200 M NaOH. Find [H₂SO₄].

1. mol NaOH = 0.00600; mol $$\ce{H2SO4}$$ = 0.00300.

**Answer:** 0.150 M.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A 48.9 mL sample of $$\ce{H2SO4}$$ is titrated to the equivalence point with 18.1 mL of 0.262 M NaOH. What is the concentration of the acid?

**Answer:** $$0.0485\ \mathrm{M}$$

**Why:** Each H₂SO₄ reacts with two OH⁻, so divide the moles of NaOH by 2.

**Working:** mol OH⁻ = 4.74 \times 10^\{-3\}; mol acid = 2.37 \times 10^\{-3\}; ÷ 0.0489 L = 0.0485 M.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$0.097\ \mathrm{M}$$: H₂SO₄ is diprotic.
- $$0.708\ \mathrm{M}$$: M_a = M_b V_b / V_a.
- $$0.194\ \mathrm{M}$$: Check the mole ratio.
