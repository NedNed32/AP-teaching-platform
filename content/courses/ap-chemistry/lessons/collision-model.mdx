A successful collision requires:

1. sufficient energy (at least $$E_a$$);
2. proper orientation.

Increasing temperature shifts the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution so a larger fraction of collisions exceeds $$E_a$$; this effect matters more than the increase in collision frequency.

<Note title="Energy distribution">
On a Maxwell–Boltzmann graph, shade the area beyond Eₐ: it grows with temperature.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Explain">
Why does a 10 °C rise often roughly double a reaction rate?

1. The fraction of collisions with energy above Eₐ increases substantially.

**Answer:** many more collisions have enough energy to react.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the facts and ideas the practice exams ask about this topic, each with the answer and the reason. If you can answer all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Facts and ideas the exams test

Cover the answers and test yourself.

**Q.** For the elementary step 2NO + Cl₂ → 2NOCl, what is the rate law?

**A.** $$\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO}]^2[\ce{Cl2}]$$. Elementary steps use their coefficients as exponents.

**Q.** How does a catalyst increase the rate of a reaction?

**A.** It provides a pathway with a lower activation energy. The reaction goes by a different mechanism.

**Q.** In a mechanism, a species that is produced in one step and consumed in a later step is a(n):

**A.** Intermediate. It does not appear in the overall equation.

**Q.** Why does increasing temperature increase reaction rate?

**A.** A larger fraction of collisions have energy at or above the activation energy. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shifts.

**Q.** Mechanism: (1) NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO (slow); (2) NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂ (fast). Which rate law is consistent with it?

**A.** $$\text{rate} = k[\ce{NO2}]^2$$. The slow first step determines the rate.
