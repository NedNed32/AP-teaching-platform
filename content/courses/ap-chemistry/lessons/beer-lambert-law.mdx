$$A = \varepsilon b c$$

- $$\varepsilon$$: molar absorptivity (depends on the substance and wavelength)
- $$b$$: path length (cm)
- $$c$$: concentration (M)

A calibration curve of known standards gives a straight line; read the unknown's concentration from its absorbance.

Errors: fingerprints or bubbles in the cuvette raise measured absorbance, making calculated concentration too high.

<Note title="Error analysis">
Think about whether an error makes absorbance read high or low, then how that affects the calculated concentration.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Find concentration">
A solution has absorbance 0.45 in a 1.0 cm cell; ε = 150 M⁻¹cm⁻¹. Find c.

1. $$c = A/(\varepsilon b) = 0.45/150$$.

**Answer:** $$3.0 \times 10^{-3}$$ M.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A solution has an absorbance of 0.688 in a 2 cm cuvette. The molar absorptivity is 181 M⁻¹cm⁻¹. What is the concentration?

**Answer:** $$1.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{M}$$

**Why:** c = A/(εb).

**Working:** c = 0.688/(181 × 2) = 1.9 \times 10^\{-3\} M.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$249\ \mathrm{M}$$: Divide A by εb.
- $$3.8 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{M}$$: Include the path length.
- $$526\ \mathrm{M}$$: The ratio is inverted.
