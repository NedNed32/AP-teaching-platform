A **free-body diagram** isolates one object and shows every force on it, each drawn from the object with its direction.

Common forces:

- weight $$mg$$, down;
- normal force $$F_N$$, perpendicular to the surface;
- tension $$F_T$$, along a string, away from the object;
- friction, parallel to the surface;
- spring force, toward the spring's equilibrium.

Choose axes aligned with the acceleration (often along an incline) and resolve forces into components.

<Note title="No ma arrow">
Do not draw ma or a net-force arrow on a free-body diagram; it shows the individual forces only.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Normal force on an incline">
A 4.0 kg block rests on a frictionless 30° incline. Find the normal force.

1. Perpendicular to the incline: $$F_N = mg\cos 30°$$.
2. $$F_N = 4.0(9.8)(0.866) = 34$$ N.

**Answer:** about 34 N.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A 83 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator that is accelerating downward at 2 m/s². What does the scale read?

**Answer:** $$647\ \mathrm{N}$$

**Why:** The scale reads the normal force: N − mg = ma (up positive).

**Working:** N = m(g − a) = 647 N.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$813\ \mathrm{N}$$: The acceleration changes the normal force.
- $$979\ \mathrm{N}$$: Check the direction of the acceleration.
- $$166\ \mathrm{N}$$: Include gravity.
