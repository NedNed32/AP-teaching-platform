$$W = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\tau\,d\theta \qquad W = \tau\Delta\theta\ \text{(constant torque)} \qquad P = \tau\omega$$

The rotational work–energy theorem: $$W_{net} = \Delta K_{rot}$$.

The area under a torque–angle graph is the work done.

<Note title="Engines">
Engine power is torque times angular speed. High torque at low rpm and lower torque at high rpm can give the same power.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Spinning up">
A 6.0 N·m torque acts on a wheel (I = 0.50 kg·m²) at rest through 4.0 revolutions. Find ω.

1. $$W = 6.0 \times 4.0(2\pi) = 151$$ J.
2. $$\tfrac12(0.50)\omega^2 = 151$$, so $$\omega = 24.6$$ rad/s.

**Answer:** about 25 rad/s.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A constant torque of 19 N·m acts on a wheel ($$I = 0.9$$ kg·m²) initially at rest, through 1 revolution. What is the wheel's final angular speed?

**Answer:** $$16.3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

**Why:** Work τΔθ (in radians) equals ½Iω².

**Working:** W = 19·1·2π = 119 J; ω = √(2W/I) = 16.3 rad/s.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$6.5\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: Convert revolutions to radians.
- $$133\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: ½Iω² = W; solve for ω.
- $$11.5\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: W = ½Iω².
