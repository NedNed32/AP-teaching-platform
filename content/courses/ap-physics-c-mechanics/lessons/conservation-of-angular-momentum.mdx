If $$\sum \tau_{ext} = 0$$: $$I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f$$.

## Rotational collisions

A clay ball sticking to the edge of a rotating disk, or a bullet hitting a hinged rod: angular momentum about the axis is conserved (the axle's force produces no torque about the axis), but linear momentum generally is not, because the axle pushes on the system.

<Note title="Kepler's second law">
A planet's angular momentum about the Sun is constant, so it moves faster when closer to the Sun.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Disk collision">
A 2.0 kg disk (R = 0.50 m) spins at 10 rad/s. A 0.50 kg lump sticks to its rim. Find the new ω.

1. $$I_i = \tfrac12(2.0)(0.25) = 0.25$$; $$I_f = 0.25 + 0.50(0.25) = 0.375$$ kg·m².
2. $$\omega_f = 0.25(10)/0.375 = 6.7$$ rad/s.

**Answer:** about 6.7 rad/s.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

### Worked exam question 1

A uniform disk of mass 4 kg and radius 0.3 m spins at 9 rad/s on a frictionless axle. A 1.5 kg lump of clay is dropped onto the rim and sticks. What is the new angular speed?

**Answer:** $$5.14\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$

**Why:** Angular momentum is conserved: I_iω_i = I_fω_f.

**Working:** I_i = 0.18, I_f = 0.315 kg·m²; ω_f = 5.14 rad/s.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- $$6.55\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: Use rotational inertias, not masses.
- $$6.8\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: Kinetic energy is not conserved in a sticking collision.
- $$2.57\ \mathrm{rad/s}$$: The clay's inertia is mR².
