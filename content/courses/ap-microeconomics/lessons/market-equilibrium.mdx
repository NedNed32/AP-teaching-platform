At equilibrium, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, and both sides of the market gain from trade. Those gains are measured as **surplus**.

## Finding equilibrium

- **Graph:** where the curves cross.
- **Table:** the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- **Equations:** set $$Q_d = Q_s$$ and solve for $$P$$, then substitute to find $$Q$$.

## Surplus

With a demand curve, a supply curve and the equilibrium price $$P^*$$:

- **Consumer surplus (CS):** triangle under demand, above $$P^*$$.
- **Producer surplus (PS):** triangle above supply, below $$P^*$$.
- **Total surplus:** $$CS + PS$$.

For straight-line curves, each surplus is a triangle: $$\frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height}$$.

## Efficiency

A competitive market at equilibrium is **allocatively efficient**: every unit whose value to buyers exceeds its cost to sellers is produced, and no other units are. Producing more or less creates a **deadweight loss**.

<Note title="Surplus is in dollars">
Surplus is measured in money, not in units. Use the price axis for the height of the triangle and the quantity axis for the base.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Surplus from equations">
Demand: $$P = 50 - Q$$. Supply: $$P = 10 + Q$$. Find equilibrium and the consumer and producer surplus.

1. $$50 - Q = 10 + Q$$ gives $$Q = 20$$ and $$P = 30$$.
2. CS: triangle with base 20 and height $$50 - 30 = 20$$: $$\frac{1}{2}(20)(20) = 200$$.
3. PS: base 20 and height $$30 - 10 = 20$$: also 200.

**Answer:** $$P = 30$$, $$Q = 20$$, CS = 200, PS = 200, total surplus 400.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

Demand is $$Q_d = 126 - 4P$$ and supply is $$Q_s = 2P - 6$$. What are the equilibrium price and quantity?

**Answer:** P = 22, Q = 38

**Why:** Set quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied.

**Working:** $$126 - 4P = -6 + 2P$$ gives $$P = 22$$, and then $$Q = 126 - 4(22) = 38$$.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- P = 24, Q = 30: At this price quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
- P = 38, Q = 22: The price and quantity are swapped.
- P = 22, Q = 126: This is the demand intercept, not the equilibrium quantity.

### Worked exam question 2

In a market with straight-line supply and demand, the equilibrium price is 12 and the equilibrium quantity is 24. The demand curve meets the price axis at 28, and the supply curve meets it at 5. What is producer surplus?

**Answer:** 84

**Why:** $$\frac{1}{2}(24)(12 - 5)$$.

**Working:** Producer surplus is the triangle above supply and below price: ½ × 24 × (12 − 5) = 84.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 192: This is consumer surplus.
- 168: Surplus is a triangle: multiply by one half.
- 276: This is total surplus.

### Worked exam question 3

In a market with straight-line supply and demand, the equilibrium price is 11 and the equilibrium quantity is 40. The demand curve meets the price axis at 52, and the supply curve meets it at 3. What is consumer surplus?

**Answer:** 820

**Why:** $$\frac{1}{2}(40)(52 - 11)$$.

**Working:** Consumer surplus is the triangle below demand and above price: ½ × 40 × (52 − 11) = 820.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- 160: This is producer surplus.
- 1,640: Surplus is a triangle: multiply by one half.
- 980: This is total surplus.

**Q.** In the market for restaurant meals, consumer incomes rise and restaurant meals are a normal good. What happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of restaurant meals?

**A.** Price will increase; quantity will increase. Higher income raises demand for a normal good. Higher income raises demand for a normal good: demand increases. A demand shift moves price and quantity in the same direction.

**Q.** In a competitive market, demand increases and, at the same time, supply increases. Which of the following describes the change in equilibrium?

**A.** Price cannot be determined; quantity will increase. Shift each curve separately: one variable moves the same way in both cases, the other depends on the sizes of the shifts. The demand shift alone moves price up and quantity up. The supply shift alone moves price down and quantity up. Where the effects agree the result is certain; where they conflict it is indeterminate.
