## Beliefs, feelings and actions toward groups

| Term | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| Stereotype | a generalized belief about a group (thought) |
| Prejudice | an unjustified negative attitude (feeling) |
| Discrimination | unjustified negative behavior (action) |

Stereotypes reduce mental effort but lead to errors. **Implicit attitudes** operate without awareness.

## Why bias persists

- **In-group bias**: we favor our own group.
- **Out-group homogeneity bias**: we see members of other groups as all alike.
- **Ethnocentrism**: judging other cultures by our own.
- The **just-world phenomenon**: believing people get what they deserve, which leads to blaming victims.
- **Belief perseverance**: clinging to a belief after it is discredited.
- **Confirmation bias**: looking for evidence that agrees with us.

## Changing attitudes

**Cognitive dissonance** is the tension we feel when our actions and attitudes clash. Since the action is already done, we usually change the attitude. In a classic study, people paid only one dollar to say a dull task was fun came to believe it was enjoyable, because the small payment did not justify the lie.

## Bias in research

Researchers guard against **social desirability bias** and **self-report bias** in participants, and against **experimenter bias**, **hindsight bias** and **overconfidence** in themselves.

<Note title="Thought, feeling, action">
Stereotype is what you think, prejudice is what you feel, discrimination is what you do.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Explain the change">
Maria thinks cheating is wrong but copies a friend's homework. Afterward she says, 'Homework does not really matter anyway.' Explain.

1. Her action conflicts with her attitude.
2. The conflict is uncomfortable.
3. She changes the attitude to reduce the discomfort.

**Answer:** Cognitive dissonance.
</WorkedExample>

### Free-response practice: Article analysis: a study of intrinsic motivation to draw

**Introduction.** Researchers investigated whether an expected reward reduces children's later interest in drawing. They hypothesized that an expected reward would affect intrinsic motivation to draw.

**Participants.** The sample was 60 preschool children who enjoyed drawing, recruited from one city. Parents gave informed consent, each child gave assent, and the plan was approved by an institutional review board. Families could withdraw at any time.

**Method.** The children were randomly assigned to one of two conditions. Children in one condition were promised a certificate for drawing and received it. Children in the other condition drew with no reward mentioned. The researchers recorded the number of minutes that each child chose to spend drawing during free time one week later.

**Results.** The group that was promised a certificate: mean minutes spent drawing = 6.5. The group that drew without a reward: mean minutes spent drawing = 13.8. The difference was statistically significant (p = 0.01).

To earn the point: States how the variable was measured in this study. Model answer: Intrinsic motivation to draw was defined as the number of minutes that each child chose to spend drawing during free time one week later.

**(c)** Describe the meaning of the difference between the two group averages.

To earn the point: Accurately describes which group scored higher or lower and what that means for the variable measured. Model answer: The group that was promised a certificate later spent less time drawing. On average, the value was 6.5 for the group that was promised a certificate and 13.8 for the group that drew without a reward, and a p value of 0.01 means a difference this large is unlikely to be due to chance.

To earn the point: Identifies an ethical guideline that the summary shows was followed. Model answer: Informed consent and assent: parents consented and each child agreed to take part. (Also acceptable: approval by an institutional review board; the right to withdraw.)
