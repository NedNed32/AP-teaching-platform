A supply chain links **suppliers → manufacturers → distributors → retailers → customers**.

## Decisions

- **Make or buy**: produce in-house or outsource.
- **Local or global** suppliers: cost versus speed and risk.
- **Inventory**: just-in-time (lean) versus safety stock (resilient).
- **Single or multiple** suppliers.

Supply chains affect competitive advantage: low costs support cost leadership; fast, flexible supply supports differentiation. Disruptions (natural disasters, shipping delays, political events) show the value of resilience.

<Note title="Ethics in the supply chain">
Businesses are increasingly held responsible for labor and environmental practices of their suppliers.
</Note>

<WorkedExample title="Trade-off">
A toy company can buy parts overseas at $2 each with a 6-week lead time, or locally at $2.60 with a 1-week lead time. Why might it choose local?

1. Shorter lead time reduces inventory needs and disruption risk.
2. It can react faster to demand changes.

**Answer:** faster, more reliable supply may be worth the higher unit cost, especially for seasonal products.
</WorkedExample>

## On the practice exams

These are the kinds of questions the practice exams ask about this topic. Each has a full answer, the reasoning, and the reason each wrong option is wrong. If you can do all of them without looking, you are ready for this topic on the exams.

### Worked exam question 1

A business needs 2,000 units. Supplier A charges $2.98 per unit plus $3,100 shipping. Supplier B charges $3.50 per unit plus $500 shipping. Which is cheaper in total, and by how much?

**Answer:** Supplier B, by $1,560

**Why:** Totals: A $9,060, B $7,500.

**Working:** A: 2000 × $2.98 + $3,100 = $9,060. B: 2000 × $3.50 + $500 = $7,500.

**Mistakes to avoid**

- Supplier A, by $1,560: Compare the full totals, including shipping.
- Supplier A, because its unit price is lower: Shipping can outweigh a lower unit price.
- Supplier B, by $1,040: Include the shipping charges.
